\section{Introduction}

\textcite{Nakamoto2008} introduced Bitcoin as a mechanism enabling peer-to-peer decentralized electronic payments, without relying on financial institutions for transaction validation. In practice, this decentralised peer-to-peer ledger, record of ownership and transfers of value, is maintained by a network of independent nodes -- computers running the protocol -- that organize transactions into blocks -- batches identified by a hash --, each pointing to the previous one. To function, validators -- the nodes responsible for verifying blocks -- must reach consensus on a unique, shared version of this ledger. Consensus can only be achieved if nodes follow a given sequence of actions needed to maintain the blockchain: the protocol (\cite{AmoussouGuenou2026}).

Validators play a critical role in order for this system to function without any central authority. They must be incentivized to fulfill their role, and to do so while ensuring compliance with the protocol. With no global oversight, incentive compatibility -- a combination of rewards (newly issued tokens distributed to validators) and penalties (loss of computing resources or staked capital) typically favors rational alignment over deviation. In Bitcoin's Proof-of-Work (PoW) consensus mechanism, entry as a validator is free and characterized by competition among nodes trying to propose the next block by solving a computation-intensive puzzle, with the odds of winning this race weighted by computational power. The winning node is rewarded while an invalid block is rejected by other nodes, sinking computational effort and energy expenditure. \textcite{Biais2019} show that PoW constitutes an equilibrium, although it is not the unique one. \textcite{AmoussouGuenou2024} obtain a reciprocal result for committee-based Proof-of-Stake (PoS) protocols.

Ethereum, created in 2015, extended Bitcoin's decentralisation beyond payment to smart contracts: self-executing programs whose interactions are themselves recorded as transactions, allowing for a broader set of applications in decentralised finance. On September 15th, 2022, the Ethereum network underwent The Merge: its original PoW layer merged with a PoS layer that had been running in parallel since December 2020. Unlike PoW, PoS requires validators to stake their own capital to be eligible to propose blocks, with the risk of losing part of their stake if they propose blocks deviating from the protocol. The protocol then randomly selects an active validator, weighted by stake, to propose the next block, rewarding the winner in the process. This transition from computational-intensive block "mining" to staked validation cut the network's energy consumption by an estimated 99.95\%, while leaving transaction history and user holdings entirely unaffected. It also set the stage for further scalability upgrades , bringing Ethereum one step closer to its roadmap.

The Merge therefore offers a natural experiment: a structural change to the protocol of one of the two largest cryptocurrencies by market capitalization. The setting is the opportunity to study how a protocol change affects the trading behaviour of a cryptocurrency, specifically the dynamics of intra-day volatility, volume and liquidity. Bitcoin's unchanged protocol serves as a counterfactual in order to disentangle effects specific to Ethereum's transition from market-wide movements affecting both assets. \medskip

\noindent Literature shows that empirical evidence of the Merge's market effects remains limited and mixed. \textcite{Tsuyuguchi2025} use a DiD and a VAR to capture ETH-BTC dynamics: they find that daily returns and on-chain fees were not significantly affected by the Merge, while trading volumes and network activity diverged significantly post-transition. \textcite{Liu2025} reports that the change was translated by improved liquidity, reduced trading activity and decrease in intraday volatility for Ethereum relative to Bitcoin. These clashing conclusions reached from different empirical strategies motivate a closer look at the volatility-volume-liquidity relationship itself. \textcite{Jain2023} studies the impact the Merghe on on-chain fees, showing that the transition reduced fees for both blockchains. This mechanism is distinct from the exchange-level fee regime changes we observe, but are pertinent to distinguish protocol-level from exchange-level effects on trading behaviour. 

Relevant to the volatility-volume relationship central to our HAR-RV approach, \textcite{Hansen2024} confirms the intraday and weekly periodicity patterns in Bitcoin and Ethereum volume we observe in our data. Finally, \textcite{Bogousslavsky2023} equity-market perspective links order imbalance volatility to the volume-spread relationship. Although being based on equity, their framework motivates the volume-illiquidity channel we test empirically through use of Amihud, Realized Amihud (\cite{Ranaldo2022}) and Kyle's lambda.

This report is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:data} describes data sources for trading data and events used throughout our analysis. Section~\ref{sec:metrics} introduces the intraday volatility and illiquidity estimators we use, and justifies our bin size choice. Finally, Section~\ref{sec:analysis} documents market periods and exchange-level fees effects, tests for cointegration across su-periods, explores flight of capital from PoW to PoS assets, and estimate HAR-RV models linking volatility to volume, liquidity and fees.

    \subsection{Market data}

Binance, the world’s largest cryptocurrency exchange by trading volume, provides an exceptionally dense and \href{https://github.com/binance/binance-public-data}{well-documented} \href{https://data.binance.vision}{public dataset} for all of its trading pairs. For spot pairs, it includes tick-by-tick trade history and candlestick data down to a 1-second resolution dating back to 2017. For our observation window, we store aggregated trades (3.7B rows) and 1-second price data (231M rows) into a DuckDB database for Ethereum-Tether (ETHUSDT) and Bitcoin-Tether (BTCUSDT) pairs. The choice of Tether pairs is justified by the market capitalization of this stablecoin -- ranked third behind Bitcoin and Ethereum -- and the substantial historical data available for these pairs on Binance.

In theory, aggregated trade data allows us to reconstruct precise higher-level aggregations by grouping trades over intervals of duration $N$. The effectiveness of this approach however depends on the fidelity of raw trade feeds. In practice, a 100\% Pearson correlation and a zero median discrepancy between downloaded and reconstructed 1-second data confirm the reliability of this method: we use it for later bin size reconstruction. 

Unlike traditional financial markets where prices are centralized, the cryptocurrency system is characterized by its fragmentation across multiple exchanges of varying volumes, listing closely aligned prices that would otherwise offer arbitrage opportunities (\cite{Makarov2020} \cite{Crepelliere2023}). For cross-validation of our Binance dataset, we use Coinbase, the second-largest exchange by volume. Its \href{https://docs.cdp.coinbase.com/api-reference/advanced-trade-api/rest-api/public/get-public-product-candles}{API} provides 1-minute historical candlestick data, which we extract via iterated requests for both USDT and USD pairs. Coinbase's USDT and USD candlesticks are seemingly interchangeable, as underlined by a ~100\% Pearson correlation and a median absolute price difference situated below 0.05\% (below 0.3\% at the 99th percentile). However, their trading activity differs significantly: USD volumes are 25 to 35 times higher than those of USDT. We therefore select the former for subsequent inter-exchange comparisons. 

Binance-Coinbase comparison reveals a ~100\% Pearson correlation and a median price difference below 0.02\% for candlestick data. Fewer than 0.01\% of 1-minute bins closing prices exhibit a discrepancy greater than 1\%, emphasizing limited upfront inter-exchange arbitrage opportunities. Given that Binance's USDT pairs have 78 to 128 times the volume of Coinbase USD contracts, we retain the former as our default data source from now on. Other major exchanges (\href{https://support.kraken.com/articles/360047543791-downloadable-historical-market-data-time-and-sales-}{Kraken}, \href{https://public.bybit.com}{ByBit}) were evaluated as alternative data sources, but either lacked directly comparable trading pairs or provided limited historical coverage.

\section{Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

We compute intraday metrics to measure both volatility and liquidity. For each pair, metrics are derived from aggregated trade data grouped at given intraday frequencies ranging from 10ms to 1-hour bins, subsequently aggregated at a daily level. Considering the size of our dataset, loading all aggregated-trades at once would overcome physical RAM limitations for metrics computation. We hence use a day-to-day data streaming approach in order to stay within our computational capacities bounds. This process is slow -- taking over an hour -- but allows us to save daily aggregated metrics based on various bin sizes. For returns computation, we choose a close-to-close measure over an open-to-close alternative, as the latter sometimes doesn't account for price movements occurring between the close of one bin and the open of the next.

Intraday volatility is not directly observable; only prices sampled at a given frequency $\Delta \tau$ can be. For day $t$ divided into $M$ intervals $t=t_0<t_1<...<t_M$, let $r_{t_i}$ denote intraday logarithmic returns over the $i$-th interval. We consider several estimators for volatility. We first use the Realized Variance ($RV_t$, Eq.~\ref{eq:rv}) as a benchmark estimator of daily variance that converges toward integrated volatility in the absence of jumps, as $M \rightarrow \infty$. For a jump-robust alternative, we implement the Bipower Variation ($BV_t^{\left\{ 1,1 \right\}}$, Eq.~\ref{eq:bv}) introduced by \textcite{Barndorff2004}. Realized Semi-variance ($RS^+$, $RS^-$, Eq.~\ref{eq:rs}) captures variance asymmetry based on sign returns, and is computed in order to distinguish the respective contributions of upside and downside risk to total volatility (\cite{Barndorff2010}). Finally, in order to leverage full candlestick data rather than log-returns alone, we include the Garman-Klass estimator ($G\text{-}K_t$, Eq.~\ref{eq:gk}) as an efficient zero-drift variance proxy (\cite{Garman1980}), alongside the Rogers-Satchell alternative ($R\text{-}S_t$, Eq.~\ref{eq:rgs}) which provides an unbiased variance estimate in case of price trends (\cite{Rogers1991}). 
\begin{align}
RV_t&=\sum\limits^M_{i=1}r^2_{t_i}\tag{1} \label{eq:rv}\\
BV_t^{\{ 1,1 \}}&=BV_T=\frac{\pi}{2}\sum\limits^M_{j=2}|r_{t_j}||r_{t_{j-1}}| \tag{2} \label{eq:bv}\\
RS_t^{+}&=\sum\limits^M_{i=1}r^2_{t_1}\mathds{1}_{rt_i\geq0}\quad\text{and}\quad RS_t^{-}=\sum\limits^M_{i=1}r^2_{t_1}\mathds{1}_{rt_i\leq0} \tag{3} \label{eq:rs}\\
G\text{-}K_t &= 0.5\left[ \ln\left(\frac{H_t}{L_t}\right)\right]^2 - (2\ln 2-1)\left[\ln\left(\frac{C_t}{O_T}\right)\right]^2\tag{4} \label{eq:gk}\\
R\text{-}S_t &= \ln\left(\frac{H_t}{C_t}\right)\ln\left(\frac{H_t}{O_t}\right)/\ln\left(\frac{L_t}{C_t}\right)\ln\left(\frac{L_t}{O_t}\right) \tag{5} \label{eq:rgs}
\end{align} 

\vspace{0.3cm}

\noindent where $O_t, H_t, L_t, C_t$ represent open, high, low and close values for day $t$, respectively.

For illiquidity measure, we rely on three different estimators. First, Amihud's illiquidity ratio (\cite{Amihud2002}) (Eq.~\ref{eq:Amihud}) is the standard reference, measuring illiquidity as a ratio of absolute percentage return to dollar volume over a given period. Its main appeal is its simplicity and availability, requiring only price and volume data. \textcite{Ranaldo2022} recently proposed an alternative version of this ratio: the Realized Amihud (Eq.~\ref{eq:RAmihud}), exploiting intraday returns rather than a single daily return, by aggregating absolute intraday log-returns before dividing by daily volume. This refinement captures intraday variation that the traditional ratio would otherwise average out. Finally, Kyle’s lambda (\cite{Kyle1985}) (Eq.~\ref{eq:Kyle}) offers an alternative price-impact measure derived from the linear relationship between trade-level order flow and price change. Unlike the previous two illiquidity estimators, this metric can only be computed from the tick-level our dataset provides, as it requires trade-by-trade data in order to obtain signed volume. 
\begin{align}
Amihud_t&=\frac{|R_t|}{V_t}\tag{6} \label{eq:Amihud}\\
RAmihud_t&=\frac{\sum\limits^M_{i=1}|r_{t_i}|}{V_t}\tag{7} \label{eq:RAmihud}\\
\Delta p_{t_i} &= \lambda_t \cdot x_{t_i} + \varepsilon_{t_i} \tag{8} \label{eq:Kyle}
\end{align}

\vspace{0.3cm}

\noindent where $R_t$ is daily return and $V_t$ is the daily dollar volume.

Signature plots (Fig.~\ref{fig:signaturePlot}) allow us to observe the evolution of our metrics as bin size increases from 10ms to 1h. Microstructure noise is noticeable at the 10ms and 100ms intervals for return-based volatility metrics, while Garman-Klass and Rogers-Satchell -- which rely on OHLC prices -- are less affected by sampling size at these frequencies. A convergence emerges from 1s onward, with all volatility metrics giving similar readings at the 5m interval. This suggests that at such granularity, choice of estimator matters little as they yield comparable results for later analysis (this high correlation is confirmed by our heatmap analysis (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendixHeatmap})). For sided metrics, realized semi-variance shows no marked asymmetry, consistent with rather balanced sided price movements.

We therefore select 5m as the default bin size for subsequent plots and analysis, in a trade off between granularity fine enough to capture true volatility, while remaining unaffected by microstructure noise. This follows \textcite{Andersen2001} who argues that for a liquid market, 5m sampling interval is optimal. \textcite{Bandi2006}, \textcite{Andersen2007}, \textcite{Patton2011} and \textcite{Haugom2014} also chose 5m as their optimal sampling frequency. For liquidity metrics, Amihud ratio is computed on a daily basis and Kyle's lambda is obtained through trade-level data: these metrics hence remain unaffected by bin size. Only Realized Amihud shows a clear decreasing trend as bin size increases. This can be explained by an economic interpretation: increasing bin size means mechanically increases volume per bin, hence reducing the market impact of normal-sized orders on markets where daily volume is measured in billions. With estimators and in size choice established, we now turn to exploring market dynamics around the Merge period.

        \subsubsection{Cointegration testing}

Cointegration allows to verify if two assets share a long-term equilibrium relation while individual prices remain non-stationary. We use the Engle-Granger test (\cite{Engle1987}) which regresses one price series on the other and tests the residuals for stationarity via an Augmented Dickey-Fuller test: 
\begin{align}
y_t &= \alpha + \beta x_t + u_t \tag{9}\\
\Delta \hat u_t &= \rho \hat u_{t-1} + \sum_{i=1}^k \phi_i \Delta \hat u_{t-i} + \varepsilon_t \tag{10}
\end{align}

\noindent with $H_0:\rho=0$ (no cointegration). This approach depends on which variable is chosen as a regressor and only handles a single cointegration relation. We hence complement it using the Johansen trace test (\cite{Johansen1988}), based on a vector error-correction representation, symmetric by construction:
\begin{align}
\Delta Y_t &= \Pi Y_{t-1} + \sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\Gamma_i \Delta Y_{t-i} + \varepsilon_t \tag{11}
\end{align}

\noindent where $\Pi=\alpha\beta '$ and rank $(\Pi)=r$ gives the number of cointegrating vectors, tested via
\begin{align}
\lambda_{trace}(r) = -T\sum_{i=r+1}^{n}\ln(1-\hat\lambda_i) \tag{12}
\end{align}

\noindent with $\hat{\lambda_i}$ the ordered eigenvalues of $\Pi$. 

According to both tests, the full sample presents no significant cointegration (Table~\ref{tab:cointegration}) despite a 0.81 correlation. This suggests that this comovement doesn't reflect a stable long-run equilibrium relationship, that might be punctuated by asymmetric shocks. This observation is evidenced in our event plots (Figure~\ref{fig:events}): despite over half of news being shared by both pairs, many events impacting Ethereum are missing from the Bitcoin plot, and the opposite holds true. For subperiods, the first one shows significant cointegration according to Engle-Granger (5\%), Johansen rank 0 (1\%) and Johansen rank 1 (5\%) tests, also highlighted by a 94\% correlation. Cointegration weakens but remains present over period 2, emphasized by significant Engle-Granger (5\%) and Johansen rank 0 (10\%) tests. Period 3 presents no cointegration and a 0.62 correlation. This sub-period evolution could be coherent with a "Bitcoin decoupling" hypothesis that can be drawn from events of this period. The arrival of crypto-ETFs as early as January of 2024 for Bitcoin and only six months later -- in July of 2024 -- for Ethereum might have led to a temporary mismatch in investment vehicles, with financial institutions trading only on the asset that is available to them, leading to diverging market behavior.

        \subsubsection{Flight of capital from PoW to PoS}

We hypothesize that the Merge might have initiated, or been preceded by, a flight of capital from networks that underwent the PoW-to-PoS transition towards networks that remained PoW throughout this period. Ethereum falls in the former category while Bitcoin fits the latter, yet we also download Binance data for this period's top cryptocurrencies (BNB, XRP, DOGE, ADA, MATIC, DOT, LTC, SOL, SHIB, TRX, UNI, AVAX, BUSD). We obtain historical weekly market capitalization from \href{https://coinmarketcap.com/historical/}{CoinMarketCap's Cryptocurrency Historical Data Snapshot}, ranging from 2022-07-03 to 2022-11-27. We cover the entirety of the Merge period: before \href{https://blog.ethereum.org/2022/08/12/finalized-no-36}{the first Merge announcement} on 2022-08-12, throughout, and after the Merge (2022-09-15). We also base our analysis on consensus mechanism evolution for these coins, obtained as of November 27, 2022 through \textcite{AmoussouGuenou2024} supplementary appendix. 

A visual study of volume and price for coins with varying consensus mechanism indicates no significant shift around the Merge period. Using a slope chart of market capitalization, we check for any relation between market capitalization and consensus mechanism throughout the Merge. Contrary to our hypothesis, no common trends are observed for coins that remained either PoW or PoS, nor for those that underwent the PoW-to-PoS transition during this period. Coins seemingly all move together from one week to another independent of consensus mechanism, prompting us not to explore the dynamics of other coins any further.

    \subsection{HAR-RV Model}
    \label{sec:harrv}
        \subsubsection{Model design and adaptation to cryptocurrrency characteristics}

To explore the dynamics linking volatility to volume, fees and liquidity, we use the Heterogeneous Autoregressive model of Realized Volatility (HAR-RV) introduced by \textcite{Corsi2009} to study how these variables affect models' performances. HAR-RV is a three factor stochastic volatility model with a simple autoregressive structure enabling treatment of cascading volatilities realized over intervals of respectively one day, one week and one month. According to \textcite{Andersen2007}, it has shown remarkably good forecasting performance comparable to more complicated long-memory ARFIMA model since its varying horizons represent fluctuating expectations over different timeframes for next period's volatility. Realized volatility over a period of $x$ trading days is defined as the average of daily quantities
\begin{align}
RV_t^{(x)}&=\frac{1}{x}\left( RV^{(d)}_t + RV^{(d)}_{t-1d} + ... +  RV^{(d)}_{t-x-1d} \right) \tag{13}
\end{align}

\noindent where RV is the realized variance -- also referred to as realized volatility -- defined in Eq.~\ref{eq:rv}. The empirical HAR-RV model is specified as follows:
\begin{align}
RV_{t+1}&=c+\beta^{(d)}RV_t^{(1)}+\beta^{(w)}RV_t^{(5)}+\beta^{(m)}RV_t^{(20)}+e_{t+1d} \tag{14} \label{eq:HAR-RV}
\end{align}

A benefit of having such a simple model is that it can easily be extended by adding additional regressors, such as lagged trading volume. \textcite{Tseng2015} introduced volume in the model, following the intuition of \textcite{Clark1973} who states that both traded volume and volatility are driven by the same underlying "news" variable and will therefore be positively correlated. This relationship has been widely documented for stock markets, for instance by \textcite{Gallant1992} and \textcite{Andersen1996}. We also extend the model using illiquidity metrics, cross-asset volatility and Binance's fees regime in order to observe how these features impact model's performance. An illiquid market is expected to be more volatile, since each order has a larger market-impact. Given the cointegration results discussed earlier, cross-asset volatility is expected to be significant between Ethereum and Bitcoin, as they tend to move together. Finally, the increased trading volume induced by the temporary removal of fees on Binance is expected to have a positive effect on volatility, as it reduces trading friction and encourages more frequent trading.

Unlike the 252 trading days observed on traditional financial markets, crypto trading happens live around the clock, with no weekend or holidays break. This characteristic prompts us to consider an adapted version of the HAR-RV model: instead of defining Eq. 13 over a week of five trading days and a month of twenty, we consider the case of markets on which trading happens 24/7 and for which a week consists of seven days and a month of thirty. 

However, Figure~\ref{fig:volume} shows that there is a clear intra-day and weekly cycle of trading volume on Bitcoin and Ethereum pairs for both Binance and Coinbase data: midday hours show higher volatility, while weekends are characterized by limited volume. We hence prefer a classical 1-5-20 configuration over a 1-7-30 model, because our data indicates that major crypto currencies volume follows the same investor attention span as for traditional financial markets. This is consistent with cycles described by \textcite{Hansen2024} in its Figure 3 and Figure 6.

        \subsubsection{Additive HAR-RV Specifications}

Table~\ref{tab:coefs} reports adjusted $R^2$ of features added individually to the base HAR-RV model, fitted over the full sample starting at the 21st day of each sub-period -- aligned with the 20-days HAR-RV requirement. The fee dummy variable equals 1 in the no-fee period, 0 otherwise. The jump component $jt$ follows \textcite{Andersen2007}, as:
\begin{align}
jt_t(\Delta)= \max{(RV_t-BV_t, 0)} \tag{15} \label{eq:jump}
\end{align}

\noindent adding it to the base model reproduces their HAR-RV-J specification. Except for cross-asset volatility, jump component and dummy variables, features are added as three cascading regressors based on the daily, weekly and monthly averages, following the different timeframes of the original model. 

For Ethereum, only lagged volume and Kyle's $\lambda$ show meaningful individual improvement with individual adjusted $R^2$ of .2148 and .2025 respectively, compared to .198 for the base model. Volume coefficients are positive but marginal and not significant, while only monthly Kyle's lambda coefficient is significant (.3948**).         \subsubsection{Proxy and loss functions for volatility forecasting}

The evaluation of volatility forecasts is hindered by the fact that the variable of interest itself -- conditional variance -- is never directly observed. \textcite{Patton2011} shows that the choice of a proxy matters in this case, as certain loss functions can lead to distorted ranking of competing forecasts. He derives conditions for a loss function to be "robust" to such distortion and shows that only mean squared-error (MSE, Eq.~\ref{eq:MSE}) and QLIKE (Eq.~\ref{eq:QLIKE}) loss functions satisfy them.
\begin{align}
    L_{MSE}(\hat{\sigma}^2,h)&=(\hat{\sigma}^2-h) \tag{16} \label{eq:MSE}\\
    L_{QLIKE}(\hat{\sigma}^2,h)&=\log h+\frac{\hat{\sigma}^2}{h} \tag{17} \label{eq:QLIKE}
\end{align}

Between those candidates, we select QLIKE over MSE for two reasons presented by \textcite{Patton2011}. First, \textcite{Patton2009} shows that Diebold-Mariano-West tests of equal predictive accuracy -- which verifies whether one forecast is significantly more accurate than another, on average --  have higher power using QLIKE loss than under MSE loss. Second, since QLIKE uses standardized error $\hat{\sigma}^2 / h$ rather than raw error $\hat{\sigma}^2-h$ of MSE, it is less sensitive to extreme observations, which is relevant in our case since heterogeneous volatility regimes are observed depending on period. Results are therefore presented using the QLIKE loss over the MSE. As for volatility proxy, we retain realized volatility (Eq.~\ref{eq:rv}) rather than other proxies such as bipower variation (Eq.~\ref{eq:bv}): in our setting, the jump component (Eq.~\ref{eq:jump}) implies that BV would be a biased proxy whenever jumps are present, and would hence violate the assumption A1 required for Patton's robustness result. Moreover, all specifications are trained to predict $RV_{t+1}$ along standard HAR-RV recommendations (\cite{Corsi2009}), so RV is the natural choice for out-of-sample evaluation: using BV would introduce a mismatch between estimation and evaluation targets that could differ in scale. 

For each specification, we compute one-step-ahead out-of-sample forecasts of $RV_{t+1}$. We use an expanding window to estimate our coefficients: starting from an initial training set covering 80\% of the relevant chronological period, the model is re-estimated at each step $t$ using all data available up to $t$. Window is then extended by one observation and the procedure is repeated for the remaining 20\% of out-of-sample data. We then compute the QLIKE loss function for each forecast and report the average over the entire period. We experiment with individual variables added to the base HAR-RV model, as well as additive models -- they aren't shown here as they didn't have the highest out-of-sample performance. We also experiment with models regressing volume and Kyle's lambda alone on realized volatility, without the base HAR-RV component, although we follow the cascading structure over daily, weekly and monthly averages of Eq.~\ref{eq:HAR-RV}. Results are reported in Table~\ref{tab:forecast}.

\section{Conclusion}

This report explores whether Ethereum transition from PoW to PoS had an impact on spot trading dynamics, using Bitcoin as a counterfactual. The Merge itself is found to be largely irrelevant to trading behaviour: no clear related effect is identified across event analysis, cointegration testing, PoW-to-PoS capital flight hypothesis and HAR-RV modelling. On the other hand, Binance's temporary removal of trading fees -- a promotional stunt that happened shortly after the Merge -- impacted trading metrics -- notably volume -- more than the protocol upgrade on its competing cryptocurrency. This is a positive finding for the Ethereum network: the Merge achieved an energy-efficient and scalable protocol, safer according to \textcite{John2025}, without disrupting trading activity. 

We also find that Ethereum-Bitcoin cointegration is unstable over event-related sub-periods: present only before the 2024 introduction of Bitcoin spot ETFs, it suggests a decoupling between the two cryptocurrencies after this event. A basic visual analysis shows no evidence of capital flight between PoW and PoS cryptocurrencies around the Merge. In the HAR-RV model, lagged volume and Kyle's lambda are the strongest individual regressors for both assets. They often outperform the full additive specifications when regressed alone against realized volatility, respectively in sub-periods of higher and lower volatility. On the data side, our day-by-day streaming approach allowed us to compute billions of aggregated trades needed for Kyle's lambda computation, and our bin reconstruction fits counterfactual Coinbase prices. 

This remains an exploratory analysis: we do not estimate a difference-in-difference model, hence why our conclusions are descriptive and further modelling would be needed for causal results.
